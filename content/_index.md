Great products are built by [normal engineers working in healthy teams](https://charity.wtf/2025/06/19/in-praise-of-normal-engineers), not heroes.

> A truly great engineering organization is one where perfectly normal, workaday software engineers, with decent software engineering skills and an ordinary amount of expertise, can consistently move fast, ship code, respond to users, understand the systems they've built, and move the business forward a little bit more, day by day, week by week.

— [Charity Majors](https://charity.wtf/2025/06/19/in-praise-of-normal-engineers)

Most of how a team actually works never gets said out loud, and new people are left to guess at it. We'd rather write it down, like other great engineering teams do:

- [37signals publishes its employee handbook](https://basecamp.com/handbook).
- [Google publishes ten things it knows to be true](https://about.google/philosophy/).
- [Netflix publishes its culture memo](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture).

Each principle below is a quotation from people who have run great engineering teams, linked to where they said it. Many come from [David Heinemeier Hansson](https://dhh.dk/), creator of Ruby on Rails and co-owner & CTO of 37signals (Basecamp & HEY). Our only real contribution was picking which ones to live by.

You'll notice there are principles here but no rules. That's on purpose. Netflix's entire vacation policy is two words, "take vacation", and it works because they assume they've hired adults who will act sensibly. We make the same assumption, and it's the thread running through everything below: engineers are trusted adults, not ["resources"](https://philosopherdeveloper.com/posts/people-are-not-resources.html). Adults direct their own work, say what they think, disagree and then commit, and own their mistakes.

This page is never really done. If a principle no longer feels right, or something's missing, open a merge request.

## 1. Manager of one

> A manager of one is someone who comes up with their own goals and executes them. They don't need heavy direction... They do what a manager would do — set the tone, assign items, determine what needs to get done, etc.

— [Basecamp](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/1430-hire-managers-of-one) · see also [*On Being a Senior Engineer*](https://www.kitchensoap.com/2012/10/25/on-being-a-senior-engineer/)

## 2. Communicate

> You can not not communicate. Not discussing the elephant in the room is communicating. Few things are as important to study, practice, and perfect as clear communication.

— [37signals](https://basecamp.com/guides/how-we-communicate)

## 3. Disagree and commit

> Consensus is cozy, but broad agreement is not our aim. The right decision is. Which is why we take the time to think, debate, persuade, listen and reconsider and then, someone, decides. If you disagree, that's fine, but once the decision is made, it's time to commit and support it completely.

— [37signals](https://37signals.com/25)

## 4. Writing solidifies, chat dissolves

> Substantial decisions start and end with an exchange of complete thoughts, not one-line-at-a-time jousts. If it's important, critical, or fundamental, write it up, don't chat it down.

— [37signals](https://basecamp.com/guides/how-we-communicate) · see also [*Scaling Engineering Teams via Writing Things Down*](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/scaling-engineering-teams-via-writing-things-down-rfcs/)

## 5. No room for ego

> Understand that sometimes your ideas will be overruled. Even if you do turn out to be right, don't take revenge or say, "I told you so" more than a few times at most, and don't make your dearly departed idea a martyr or rallying cry.

— [*The Ten Commandments of Egoless Programming*](https://blog.codinghorror.com/the-ten-commandments-of-egoless-programming/)

## 6. Protect your time

> We limit ourselves to a 40-hour work week. Keeping our hours at work limited forces us to prioritize the work that really matters... There are occasions when teams or individuals need to work off-hours for on-call, maintenance, or emergencies. This time should not be in addition to your normal working hours. Use your discretion to take time off to make up for the additional hours you put in during the week.

— [37signals](https://basecamp.com/handbook/how-we-work)

## 7. Psychological safety and high trust

> Individuals on teams with higher psychological safety... feel safe taking risks in the face of being seen as ignorant, incompetent, negative, or disruptive, confident that no one on the team will embarrass or punish anyone else for admitting a mistake, asking a question, or offering a new idea.

— [Google re:Work, Project Aristotle](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness)

## How we check team health

Principles only mean something if you check whether you're living up to them, so we periodically score ourselves from 1 to 5 on three questions. [Spotify's squad health check](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2014/9/squad-health-check-model) uses ten; we borrowed the format and kept only the three that Google's [Project Aristotle](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness) found actually predict team effectiveness. Three is also short enough that the check doesn't become a chore.

| Question | 1 | 5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Suitable process.** Does our way of working fit us, or do we do things just because we've always done them? | We do things because we've always done them; the process gets in our way. | Our way of working fits us, and we change it when it doesn't. |
| **Team trust and collaboration.** Do we work as one team, help each other, and feel safe admitting mistakes and asking "dumb" questions? | Individuals protect turf; mistakes get held against people. | One team; help freely given; safe to be wrong and ask anything. |
| **Clarity of success.** Do we know what success looks like and how it's measured? | We don't know what success looks like or how it's measured. | We know exactly what success looks like and track it. |
